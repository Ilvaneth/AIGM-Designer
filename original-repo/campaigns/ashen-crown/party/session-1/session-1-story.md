# Session 1 — What the Ash Remembers

Mother Vey did not waste words, and that was exactly why Ilvaneth Duskmere trusted her more than most people who used twice as many.

"Sit," the old fence said, checking the bolt on her back-room door before she'd even finished the word. A pouch landed on the table between them — light, but full. "There's a mahzen under this town. Sealed. I want it opened before the Concordat gets here and does it *properly*." She said the word like it tasted bad. "Whatever's inside is yours. I just want to know the seal broke."

Three, maybe four days, she said. That was the window.

Kriv Shestendeliath didn't touch the pouch until Ilvaneth had already pocketed it — old habit, not distrust. Trust was not a thing either of them extended carelessly, except to each other, and even that had been earned the hard way, in a yard full of other people's discarded children, a long time before either of them had learned to lie for a living.

They didn't walk into the dark blind. Ilvaneth still had people in this town who owed her conversation if nothing else, and Fennick Orle poured secrets the way other barkeeps poured ale — for a price, or an old debt, or sometimes just because he liked being the only one who knew something. Tonight it was old debt, dressed up as flattery. He gave them what he had: the convoy was real, it was close, and someone else — a fixer called the Grey Tally — had been circling the same mahzen for days, working for money that smelled like the Concordat. And then, almost as an afterthought, a story about ash-kings, and a grave meant to keep something from coming back.

The seal on the mahzen door told its own story, if you knew how to read it. Ilvaneth did. This wasn't a lock built to keep thieves out — it was a binding, tended and renewed by hands she never saw, built to keep something *in*. She found the place where the pattern gave, worked it loose without waking whatever slept on the other side, and the door opened on a room that had been waiting a very long time for someone to ask it the right question.

The guardian was real. Armored, ash-colored, seated in a niche like a king who'd forgotten how to stand — and breathing, faintly, in a rhythm that had nothing to do with being alive. It never stirred. Kriv kept his eyes on it from the stairs while Ilvaneth crossed the room on feet that made no sound at all, and found, on the chest's lock, a plate that wasn't waiting to be forced. It was waiting to be given something.

She thought about the word the magic had shown her — not fire, but what fire leaves behind — and looked at the urns lining the walls, still full of centuries-old ash. She poured a handful over the plate and held her breath.

The ward accepted it. The guardian did not wake.

Inside: a shard of something broken, ash-grey, warm without a source, the kind of object that pulled the eye back to it even when you tried to look away. Alongside it, smaller things — a tarnished bracelet, a few old coins, a bone bead necklace — the unremarkable dead of a much older grave. Worn letters near the guardian's niche spoke of a vow, and a return, in words neither of them could fully place. And outside, in the dirt by the entrance, a second set of footprints that weren't theirs — close, recent, and turned back before they'd ever gone in.

They didn't chase whoever had left them. Not tonight.

Vey took the news for what it was worth, which was less than the truth. *A guardian, they said. Scared us off.* She didn't call the lie by its name — she didn't have to. Something in the too-clean shape of the story stayed with her, filed away for later, the way she filed everything.

"Gallowmere's small," she told them, before they left. "If you're keeping something, I'll know eventually."

Neither of them doubted her. Neither of them cared, not tonight.

They turned in with the fragment between them, warm in the dark, and let the ash-kings' old promise wait until morning.
