### A.2 The owner's four questions

1. **Sound?** Yes at the core: the script rolls, the model writes, the door tests, the swap and order tests prove, the ledger tracks. The proofs live in the script layer; the model layer has four real births. Findings 1 and 2 sit on that line.
2. **Faults:** 1, 2, 4, 6 serious; 5, 7, 8, 9 middling; 10, 11 small.
3. **Done otherwise:** the owner never touching the secret, from the start; solo play in P0 (it is, see B.1); no mixed birth across old and new machinery; a per-topic compatibility matrix instead of a flat pair list.
4. **In general:** the strongest habit is turning every fault into a machine rule and reading the generated output. The weakest point is that the human and model layers are not measured with the script layer's rigour. Findings 3, 5, 7 and 9 recur at every floor: put them into the floor template.

### A.3 The design tab's six questions

5. **Variety against coherence.** The risk is not sameness but disconnection: legal pieces with no story between them; the tag system removes contradiction, not non-sequitur. The threat chain is the answer; carry it into P3-P6 (every site to a stage, every power to a side). Template sameness is real: "X does Y; now A and B fight over C" is recognised by the tenth campaign; roll the presentation too. Give the model the "why" between two rolled facts, sensory texture and NPC voice; give it nothing a later floor depends on. The owner has read every table and will recognise rows at the table; the surprise is in the draw and the combination.
6. **Secrecy.** The swap test is right and stays; it needs findings 1 and 2 to be complete. "The owner never approves" is too much: approving the public card is valuable and leaks nothing. Remaining surfaces: review-stop text, refusal reasons, per-agent cost, the secret writer's duration, and the `rerun` decision itself.
7. **Scaling of the tag system.** Pairs between claim values grow linearly; a flat list of hundreds will not be readable. Keep a value set and a small compatibility matrix per topic; derive the pairs; cap `requires` chains; keep "one target, one owner" and have it generate tests. Without the coverage measure (finding 5) nobody knows whether it scales.
8. **D&D completeness.** Missing from P0-P2: the party decision (B.1), the player's seat, warlock patrons and paladin oaths from the cosmos. Belonging to P3-P8 and already in the plan: the start settlement, travel and encounter tables, the map, faction clocks, the patron NPC, rumours, dungeon structure, treasure by tier, win and loss endings.
9. **Cost.** 87 k output against about 1 M context says every agent rereads a large bundle. Read bundles first; the door before the critic; critics on a cheaper tier; critics that never produced a meaningful `fix` become door rules; the phase and wishes critics in one call. Per-entity fan-out is the right shape; per-critic fan-out is not.
10. **Process.** Justified for now. Two trims: the last read and the sufficiency check for specifications and rulings only, not for a tab's question; risk-tiered audits (table-only commits get the suite and a row diff; code commits get the agent audit). The same tab writes and audits: an outside review per slice is the remedy.

- **The suite** holds 961 test functions and runs in 32-34 minutes in the clean checkout (`docs/p1-tags.md` §9), and it runs twice per item (the coding tab's run and the audit's). See C.6.

### E.2 The critics: what they do, measured

What this says:
1. **The critics' real catches are structural**, and the door now makes them: a secret sentence in a note, a dm-only id in a public field, a duplicate full name, a Turkish operation name. Every one of those is a string or id check. The one craft catch that fires, voice distinctness, is measurable too (shared tics and repeated line shapes are word statistics).
2. **The taste rubrics never fire.** The eighteen that never failed include the ones that should have caught the abstract story of the first P1 birth (legibility, "only true here", "a threat with a face"). A judge that always says pass is not a gate; it is cost.
3. **The loop always converges to pass**, and the second critic mostly agrees. Either the writer fixes everything the critic asks, or the critic concedes on the second read; the record cannot tell which. Nothing measures whether the text got better.
4. **The wishes critic has never done anything.** Its job (a must or must-not held across phases) is a fact check, not a judgement.

### E.3 A more effective shape

The principle: **the model is a sensor, not a judge.** A model is unreliable at "is this good" and reliable at "what does this say". So every check is turned into a question whose answer the script can compare with something it already knows.

| Today | Instead | What it buys |
|---|---|---|
| a critic reads the text and says pass or fix on taste rubrics | **a readback**: a blind reader (no frame, no rolls) is asked to fill a short form from the public text alone: who is the threat, what does it want, where does the first session open, what is each side's stance, which god does a cleric choose. The script compares the form with the rolls. A field the reader cannot fill is a legibility fault the writer must fix; a field it fills wrongly is a contradiction. | legibility and D&D-ness measured, not judged; the writer's fix instruction is exact ("the reader could not find the first session's task") |
| the leak rubric and the door's exact-string checks | **the guess critic**: the same blind reader gets the public text and N candidate secrets (the real one among rows of the same tables) and ranks them. Over many births the hit rate must sit at chance. A single birth's confident hit is a fix. | the only check that sees a *hinted* secret; the swap test's counterpart for the model layer |
| the phase critic reading every entity for voice, distinctness, reskins | **script measures over readback forms and word statistics**: shared speech tics, repeated line shapes, two regions with the same identity line, two NPCs whose forms match on every field | model-free, every birth, in the suite |
| the wishes critic | **a readback question per wish** ("does the text satisfy: no undead? answer yes or no and name the sentence") checked by the script, or a plain string rule where the wish is lexical | the wish is checked as a fact |
| two fix loops with a critic in each | **best of two with one comparison**, on the few documents that matter (the premise, the cosmos, later the BBEG): two writers draft, one comparing judge picks (a judge is far more reliable at "which is better" than at "is this good"); then the readback. For ordinary entities: one write, the door, the readback, one fix at most | fewer calls than write + critic + two loops, and a measurable result |
| reason codes as free slugs | a closed list per rubric | the only thing a critic returns that the owner reads |

The owner stays the only taste judge, on the card, where he reads anyway.

Cost: a readback is a short call on a cheap tier (it writes a form, not prose); the guess critic likewise; the comparison judge is one call on the documents that get two drafts. Against today's up to five model calls per entity (write, critic, fix, critic, second critic), the ordinary entity costs write + readback + at most one fix.

What to keep from today: the door (it is the thing that works), `design_critique_stats.py` (the measurement), the rubric texts as the readback's questions, the two-reader rule for the secret documents (two blind readers, not two taste critics).

What to decide first: (a) whether the readback replaces the entity critic everywhere or only on the scripted phases (P1, P2 now; the rest as they are rebuilt); (b) which documents get two drafts; (c) which tier the readers run on. The workflow loops (wave 4) are rewritten once, after this is ruled.
